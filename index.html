Main Sewer Line Inspection and Repair Guide: Get the Video First

In most US municipalities, the homeowner owns and maintains the private sewer lateral pipe running from the house foundation all the way out to the street main connection (or into a private septic tank). Because buried clay, cast-iron, or Orangeburg pipes deteriorate silently underground, an independent sewer-camera video is the essential diagnostic before approving major excavation or lining bids.

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) highlights how cracked pipes, root intrusion, and grease blockages contribute to sanitary sewer overflows and household sewage backups—and offers dedicated homeowner guidance for properties served by decentralized onsite septic systems rather than municipal sewers.

Four steps before signing a sewer-replacement contract

Pipe failure breakdowns, replacement scopes, and maintenance guides are compiled on Sewer Line Lab and its main sewage line replacement overview.

  1. Keep a copy of the recorded scope video: Always request the digital video file showing the footage counter and above-ground paint flags marking defect depth and location so second-opinion contractors can bid from the same evidence.
  2. Check your municipal lateral boundary: Some cities maintain the portion between the curb/sidewalk and the street main while others make the owner responsible all the way to the tap.
  3. Verify plumbing permits and 811 locates: Buried main-line repairs require utility marking via 811 and municipal plumbing/right-of-way permits—especially if work crosses a public sidewalk or street.
  4. Review homeowners insurance endorsements: Standard policies often exclude exterior buried service-line collapse unless a specific service-line or water-backup rider is in place.
Never pour harsh chemical drain openers down a backed-up main line—standing caustic liquid sits in the pipe and creates chemical burn hazards for any plumber who subsequently opens a cleanout or snakes the line.
